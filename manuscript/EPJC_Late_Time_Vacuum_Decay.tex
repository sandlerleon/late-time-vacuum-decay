\begin{abstract}
A metastable dark-energy vacuum could nucleate bubbles that evolve into daughter regions. We ask whether such a region can be a hot expanding universe and whether cosmological cooling raises the transition probability. For a scalar potential with a metastable minimum at the dark-energy scale we compute the Coleman--De Luccia bounce (error budget, negative-mode structure, 24 executable checks); all scales follow from the dark energy, $g = f^2/M_{\rm Pl}^2 \sim 10^{-60}$, and the quantum decay is controlled by one action $B = b(\eta)/\lambda$, with $\Gamma/H^4 = 1$ at $B \simeq 287$. Survival is evaluated on the Lorentzian past light cone of the observed expansion history ($V_4 = 0.059\,H_\Lambda^{-4}$, 445 times below the Euclidean four-sphere; 95\% bound $\Gamma/H_\Lambda^4 < 51$). A thermal mass rescales the potential exactly, with $\eta_{\rm eff} = \eta/(1-2\theta)^{3/2}$; the O(3) thermal saddle dominates above 0.18 meV and the rate falls monotonically as the universe cools, so cooling cannot raise the late-time probability: a decoupled scalar is unaffected, and one in equilibrium with the CMB raises the excluded prior mass from 15\% to 24--49\% and closes the late-time decay window (priors are samples, not probabilities). Lorentzian simulations of the daughter region with two fields, dissipation and gravity show that it reheats to at most $2.2$ meV and stays curvature dominated; a reservoir hidden by a negative offset leaves an anti-de Sitter vacuum that crunches after 5.5 s when it reaches 0.7 MeV; flattening at the dark-energy scale needs a vev above $4M_{\rm Pl}$ and still reheats below 1.5 meV; and repeated transitions obey $\sum\Delta V\le\rho_\Lambda$. A hot epoch at 1 s would need a parent energy scale at least 41 orders of magnitude above the Planck scale, and a matter trigger $\Delta\ln m \gtrsim 8.9\times10^{-4}$. The daughter region is cold and empty, and a cyclic reset by the present dark energy is not supported.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} \emph{false vacuum decay; Coleman--De Luccia instanton; thermal activation; daughter universe; dark energy; reheating; cyclic cosmology; past light cone; falsifiability}

\section{Introduction}
In the standard cosmological model the late universe approaches de Sitter space: matter and radiation dilute, and a positive vacuum energy $\rho_\Lambda$ remains. If that energy is not an exact cosmological constant but the energy of a metastable scalar vacuum, the vacuum can decay by quantum tunneling through a Coleman--De Luccia (CDL) instanton [1]. It has been suggested that such a late-time transition could reset the cosmological history, so that the long-term evolution is cyclic rather than terminal; cyclic and bouncing proposals are discussed in [2, 3, 4, 5]. Two things must be kept apart. The formation of a bubble universe is not a cyclic cosmology: a cycle needs a mechanism that connects successive epochs. The question tested here is narrower and can be answered by calculation: can the tunneling of the present dark energy produce a new hot, expanding epoch? That requires a specified metastable minimum, the tunneling action including gravity, a rate compared with the observed expansion history, and the interior of the nucleated region followed to see whether it can become a hot big bang.

A further distinction concerns the outcome. In a branching or recycling picture (Option A) a parent metastable vacuum nucleates daughter regions by tunneling; each is a bubble inside the parent spacetime, and whether it is a hot expanding universe, or a universe at all in the sense of [6, 7, 8], is a separate question. In a genuinely cyclic cosmology (Option B) the same region expands, contracts, bounces and reheats, which needs a dynamical bounce beyond tunneling and raises problems of stability and entropy [9, 10]. This paper addresses Option A with the Coleman--De Luccia theory and treats Option B only by stating, in Section 5.4, what it would require.

Four claims are tested. (i) A scalar potential with a metastable minimum at the dark-energy scale can nucleate bubbles of lower-energy vacuum at a rate that is neither negligible nor already excluded. (ii) The nucleated region reheats into a hot expanding universe. (iii) The transition is triggered, or enhanced, by the dilution of matter in the late universe. (iv) Cosmological cooling, acting on a scalar coupled to the CMB, makes the transition more probable at late times. The analysis is conditional: it takes the dark-energy density as the false-vacuum energy and asks what follows. The result is a quantitative consistency and exclusion analysis, not a new cosmological mechanism.

The contributions are the following.

\begin{itemize}\item A reproducible map from the parameters of an explicit scalar potential to cosmological decay times, with a numerical error budget, an independent boundary-value solution, the negative-mode structure of the bounce, the thin-wall, Parke and Hawking--Moss limits, and a treatment of the prefactor as a systematic uncertainty (Sections 2--4).
\item A joint analysis of nucleation, survival and the post-transition energy budget, with the survival of the present vacuum evaluated on the Lorentzian past light cone of the observed expansion history (Section 4.2), and a statement of which energy sources the reheating bound excludes by geometry and which only by energy conservation (Section 5).
\item Explicit conditions that separate a late-time vacuum transition from a viable cosmological restart, divided into internal consistency conditions and observational tests (Section 7), and a model-independent requirement on any matter coupling that could trigger the transition (Section 6).
\item A finite-temperature extension: the exact rescaling of the thermally corrected potential, the O(3) and O(4) exponents and their crossover, the past-light-cone survival integral with a time-dependent rate, and prior maps showing that cooling cannot raise the late-time transition probability (Sections 4.6--4.8).
\item Lorentzian simulations of the daughter region with two fields, dissipation and gravity, a reservoir hidden by a negative offset, hilltop flattening, repeated transitions and entropy accounting, and the resulting criteria for a viable daughter universe (Section 5.5).
\end{itemize}

\section{Scalar effective theory and the potential}
We consider a real scalar field minimally coupled to Einstein gravity,

\begin{equation}
S=\int d^4x\sqrt{-g}\left[\frac{M_{\rm Pl}^2}{2}R-\frac12 g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\varphi\,\partial_\nu\varphi-V(\varphi)\right]
\end{equation}
where $M_{\rm Pl}$ is the reduced Planck mass. The false vacuum has the energy density $V_f = V(\varphi_f)$, which is identified with the observed dark energy, $V_f = \rho_\Lambda = 2.55\times10^{-47}$ $\mathrm{GeV}^4$ (quarter-root 2.25 meV; Planck 2018 [37], $H_0 = 67.66\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.6889$). The asymptotic de Sitter rate is $H_\Lambda = \sqrt{V_f/3}/M_{\rm Pl} = 1.20\times10^{-42}$ GeV, so that $H_\Lambda^{-1} = 17.4$ Gyr, and $M_{\rm Pl}^4/V_f = 1.38\times10^{120}$, with $\ln(M_{\rm Pl}^4/V_f) = 276.6$. A microscopic origin of the potential is not assumed.

A single dimensionless family covers the generic metastable case. With $x = \varphi/f$,

\begin{equation}
V(\varphi)=V_t+f^4\lambda\left[\frac{(x^2-1)^2}{8}+\frac{\eta\,x}{2}-p_{\min}(\eta)\right],\qquad x^3-x+\eta=0\ \Rightarrow\ x_t<x_{\rm top}<x_f
\end{equation}
where $p_{\min}$ is the value of the bracket at the true minimum, so that $V(\varphi_t) = V_t$. The tilt $\eta$ lifts the degeneracy of the two wells; a false minimum exists only for $\eta < \eta_c = 2/(3\sqrt3) = 0.3849$. The energy difference and the barrier height above the false minimum are

\begin{equation}
\Delta V=V_f-V_t=f^4\lambda\,\delta(\eta),\qquad V_{\rm top}-V_f=f^4\lambda\,\beta(\eta),\qquad \delta\simeq\eta\ (\eta\ll1)
\end{equation}
The motivation for this family is minimality. A quartic with a small linear term is the simplest renormalizable potential with a metastable minimum and was the standard example in the original treatments of vacuum decay [11, 1]; it has three parameters with direct meaning, the quartic coupling $\lambda$, the tilt-to-barrier ratio $\eta$ and the scale $f$, and the latter is fixed by the dark energy. The coupling is restricted to $10^{-2}\le\lambda\le4\pi$, the upper limit being the perturbative-unitarity bound for a quartic interaction and the lower limit an arbitrary but wide cut that is varied in Section 4.5; the tilt is restricted to $0.03\le\eta\le0.38$, which excludes the nearly degenerate case, where $B\propto\eta^{-3}$ is so large that the transition is irrelevant, and ends just below the point where the minimum disappears. Other potentials change $b(\eta)$ and the interior energy fraction but not the scales that decide the results (Section 8). A scalar with a mass of the order of meV is technically unnatural without a protecting symmetry; this is a known property of every quintessence-like model and is not addressed here.

The choice $V_t = 0$ (decay to a Minkowski vacuum, $r_L \equiv V_f/\Delta V = 1$) is the baseline, because it maximizes the energy released to the interior for a given $V_f$ when $V_t\ge0$; $V_t < 0$ (anti-de Sitter) and $V_t > 0$ are considered in Sections 5.3 and 4.5. The scale $f$ is not free: it is fixed by $V_f = \rho_\Lambda$, $f^4 = \rho_\Lambda/(r_L\lambda\delta)$. For $\lambda = 1$ and $\eta = 0.3$ one finds $f = 3.05$ meV and a field excursion of $5.8$ meV, that is, the dark-energy scale. The gravitational parameter is then fixed too,

\begin{equation}
g\equiv\frac{f^2}{M_{\rm Pl}^2}=\sqrt{\frac{V_f/M_{\rm Pl}^4}{r_L\,\lambda\,\delta(\eta)}}
\end{equation}
which takes the values $6.4\times10^{-61}$ to $2.7\times10^{-60}$ over the benchmark parameters of Table 4. Gravity is therefore a $10^{-60}$ effect in every equation below that does not contain the large number $M_{\rm Pl}^4/V_f$.

\section{The Euclidean bounce, with and without gravity}
\subsection{Equations and action}
The O(4)-symmetric Euclidean metric $ds^2 = d\xi^2 + \rho(\xi)^2 d\Omega_3^2$ gives the CDL equations [1]

\begin{equation}
\varphi''+3\frac{\rho'}{\rho}\varphi'=\frac{dV}{d\varphi},\qquad \rho'^2=1+\frac{\rho^2}{3M_{\rm Pl}^2}\left(\frac12\varphi'^2-V\right)
\end{equation}
with $\varphi'(0)=0$ and regularity at the second pole of the compact de Sitter geometry. On shell, the action of the bounce relative to the false vacuum is

\begin{equation}
B=S_E[{\rm bounce}]-S_E[{\rm false}],\quad S_E=4\pi^2\int d\xi\,\left(\rho^3V-3M_{\rm Pl}^2\rho\right),\quad S_E^{\rm dS}=-\frac{24\pi^2M_{\rm Pl}^4}{V_f}
\end{equation}
In the flat-space limit $B=2\pi^2\int dr\,r^3\,[\tfrac12\varphi'^2+V-V_f]$, and for a thin wall $B = 27\pi^2\sigma^4/(2\Delta V^3)$ with $R_c = 3\sigma/\Delta V$, $\sigma = \int\sqrt{2V}\,d\varphi$ [11]. With the field in units of $f$ and lengths in units of $(\sqrt\lambda f)^{-1}$ the flat-space action is $B = b(\eta)/\lambda$ exactly, and gravity enters only through $g$.

The Hawking--Moss action is $B_{\rm HM} = 24\pi^2 M_{\rm Pl}^4(1/V_f - 1/V_{\rm top})$ [13]; an oscillating CDL bounce requires $|V''_{\rm top}| > 4H^2$ [15, 16], which fails for sufficiently flat potentials or strong gravity.

\subsection{Numerical method and validation}
The bounce is found by shooting on the central field value with overshoot/undershoot classification (adaptive eighth-order Runge--Kutta, relative tolerance $10^{-11}$) [38, 39, 40], with the equations integrated through the wall and the action accumulated along the solution. For the gravitational problem the tail of the bounce outside the wall follows the false-vacuum de Sitter geometry $\rho = \sin(H\xi)/H$ toward the second pole, and the difference from the background action is taken analytically there, which avoids the cancellation of terms of order $1/g^2$. In the thin-wall regime shooting is exponentially sensitive to the initial condition, so an independent collocation boundary-value solver is used for $\eta < 0.1$, and the two agree to $2\times10^{-9}$ where both apply. The analytic thin-wall formula with gravity [14] serves as a further check of the gravitational suppression. Table 2 lists the checks of the bounce; the complete suite has 24 checks, all executed by a script distributed with the paper.

A CDL bounce is a saddle point with exactly one negative mode, which is what allows $\mathrm{Im}\,E$ to be interpreted as a decay rate [11, 12]. For $\eta=0.05$, 0.15, 0.30 and 0.36 the spectrum of $-\nabla_4^2+V''(\varphi_b)$ was computed in the angular sectors $l=0,\dots,4$ (with $\psi=r^{-3/2}u$ the radial operator is $-u''+[(l(l+2)+3/4)/r^2+V'']u$, discretized on 30000 points). In every case the $l=0$ sector has exactly one negative eigenvalue (lowest values $-0.002$, $-0.024$, $-0.21$, $-0.74$ in units of the false-vacuum mass squared), the $l=1$ sector has the lowest eigenvalue consistent with the four translational zero modes (at most $2\times10^{-7}$ of $m_f^2$ in magnitude) and none is negative, no eigenvalue is negative for $l\ge2$, the field is monotonic, so that $\varphi-\varphi_f$ has no node, and the Derrick identity holds to $1\times10^{-7}$. With gravity the negative-mode problem is subtle and has been discussed in the literature [41]; here $g\sim10^{-60}$, the operator differs from its flat-space form by a relative $O(g)$, and the flat-space structure applies. The gravitational spectrum was not computed for large $g$.

Corrections of relative size $10^{-59}$ cannot be resolved in double precision, and the paper does not claim to resolve them. The flat-space action is computed with no gravity at all; the gravitational correction is the linear response $B=B_{\rm flat}(1-\kappa g)$ with $\kappa(g\to0)=3.641$, obtained from $g=10^{-3}$ and $2\times10^{-3}$ with the linear extrapolation $\kappa_0=2\kappa(10^{-3})-\kappa(2\times10^{-3})$ (the two values differ by 2.6\%, the curvature of $B(g)$) and stable to $3\times10^{-5}$ across integrators. At $g\sim10^{-60}$ the correction $\kappa g\sim9.7\times10^{-60}$ is some 59 orders of magnitude below every other uncertainty of the rate, so what matters is only that gravity enters as a small smooth perturbation, which the Hawking--Moss criterion confirms independently (Section 3.4).

\subsection{Gravitational corrections and the Hawking--Moss limit}
Fig. 2a shows the ratio $B(g)/B_{\rm flat}$ computed on a grid of $g$ and $\eta$. They matter only if $f\gtrsim 0.3 M_{\rm Pl}$, which with $V_f$ fixed requires $V_f/f^4\lesssim9\times10^{-119}$, a barrier $\gtrsim1\times10^{118}$ times the vacuum energy, and an action beyond any consequence for the transition.

\section{Nucleation rate, cooling and survival in the observed universe}
\subsection{The rate}
The rate per unit four-volume is $\Gamma/V_4 = A\,e^{-B}$ [11, 12]. In the rescaled variables $\Gamma/V_4 = K f^4\lambda^2 e^{-B}$, where $K = (B/2\pi)^2 D$ contains the four translational zero modes and an unknown dimensionless factor $D$ from the remaining fluctuation determinant. Dividing by $H^4 = V_f^2/(9M_{\rm Pl}^4)$ and using $f^4 = V_f/(r_L\lambda\delta)$,

\begin{equation}
\frac{\Gamma}{H^4}=\frac{9K\lambda}{r_L\,\delta(\eta)}\,\frac{M_{\rm Pl}^4}{V_f}\,e^{-B(\lambda,\eta)}
\end{equation}
The large number $M_{\rm Pl}^4/V_f = 1.38\times10^{120}$ is the number of Planck-volume cells in a Hubble four-volume. It compensates an action of $B \simeq \ln(M_{\rm Pl}^4/V_f) = 276.6$ plus the logarithm of the prefactor. Setting $\Gamma/H^4 = 1$ gives the critical action

\begin{equation}
B_c=\ln\frac{M_{\rm Pl}^4}{V_f}+\ln\frac{9K\lambda}{r_L\,\delta},\qquad B_c\simeq 287\ \ (\text{median over the prior; 5--95\% range }280\text{--}294)
\end{equation}
For a mean time to nucleation, an observer in the asymptotic de Sitter space sees the past-cone four-volume grow at the rate $(4\pi/3)H^{-3}$, so the mean time for one Hubble patch to nucleate a bubble is $t_{\rm dec} = H^{-1}/[(4\pi/3)\Gamma/H^4]$ for $\Gamma/H^4\ll1$. For $\lambda=1$, $\eta=0.30$ the action is $B=441$ and the mean decay time is $10^{76}$ yr; at $\eta=0.32$ ($B=301$) it is $10^{15}$ yr; at $\eta=0.34$ ($B=194$) the rate is so large that the vacuum would not have survived and the model is excluded (Table 4).

\subsection{Survival of the present vacuum: the past light cone}
A bubble nucleated at an event $p$ expands at essentially the speed of light and reaches an observer at $O$ if and only if $p$ lies in the causal past of $O$. If nucleations are Poisson distributed with rate $\Gamma$ per unit four-volume, the probability that none has occurred in the past light cone of the present observer is [19, 20]

\begin{equation}
P_{\rm surv}=e^{-\Gamma V_4},\qquad V_4=\frac{4\pi}{3}\int_0^{t_0}dt\,a(t)^3\chi(t)^3,\qquad \chi(t)=\int_t^{t_0}\frac{dt'}{a(t')}
\end{equation}
with $\chi$ the comoving distance of the light cone at time $t$ in a flat universe. A common order-of-magnitude shortcut uses the Euclidean volume of the de Sitter four-sphere, $8\pi^2/(3H^4)$; that is not the four-volume of any region of the observed spacetime, and it overstates $V_4$ by the factor given below. The observed history is $H^2=H_0^2(\Omega_ma^{-3}+\Omega_ra^{-4}+\Omega_\Lambda)$ with $\Omega_\Lambda=0.6889$, $\Omega_r=9.14\times10^{-5}$ (from $T_{\rm CMB}$ and $N_{\rm eff}=3.046$) and $\Omega_m=0.3110$. Two independent quadratures give $V_4=0.12457\,H_0^{-4}=0.0591\,H_\Lambda^{-4}$, agreeing to $1\times10^{-13}$; the result is converged in the lower cutoff of the scale factor and in the grid to $10^{-11}$ and reproduces the analytic Einstein--de Sitter value to $2\times10^{-13}$ (check V14). The Euclidean volume is $26.3\,H_\Lambda^{-4}$, 445 times larger. Of $V_4$, 35\% comes from events at redshift below 1, 73\% below 2 and 97\% below 5 (Fig. 3); the uncertainty from $\Omega_\Lambda$ at the Planck $1\sigma$ level is about 3.5\%.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=0.59\textwidth]{../figures/fig3_survival_cone.png}\caption{Four-volume of the past light cone of the present observer, accumulated over events at redshift below $z$, for the observed expansion history, against the Euclidean four-sphere volume.}\end{figure}
The observation that no bubble has reached us is excluded at 95\% confidence if $\Gamma V_4>-\ln0.05=3.00$, that is for $\Gamma/H^4>50.7$ or $B<B_c-3.9$. This is a weak condition: it is 1334 times weaker than the Euclidean estimate ($\Gamma/H^4>0.038$), it moves the boundary of the allowed region by only $\Delta B=7.2$ out of about 287, and it bounds the future mean decay time only by $t_{\rm dec}\gtrsim8.2\times10^{7}$ yr (the formula for $t_{\rm dec}$ is used here as an order-of-magnitude statement, since $\Gamma/H^4$ is not small). Because the prior mass sits overwhelmingly at $B\ll B_c$ or $B\gg B_c$, the fraction of the prior excluded changes from 15.0\% (Euclidean estimate) to 14.8\% (Section 4.5). The criterion assumes that $\Gamma$ was constant, with the scalar in its false minimum throughout; it does not require observers to be present before the transition, and it says nothing about bubbles nucleated outside our past cone. Percolation of bubbles in de Sitter space requires a rate of order unity in Hubble units [21, 22], since otherwise the false-vacuum volume grows without bound and no wall collides with another. With the corrected survival bound this no longer conflicts with survival: the window $1\lesssim\Gamma/H^4<51$ has the width $\Delta B=3.9$ and contains 0.12\% of the prior volume (0.18\% for a percolation threshold of 0.1 and 0.05\% for 10).

\subsection{The prefactor and the validity of the semiclassical rate}
The factor $D$ is the ratio of the fluctuation determinants with the zero modes removed; methods to compute it for specific potentials exist [17, 18] and are not repeated here. It is treated as a systematic uncertainty. Because $\ln D$ enters $B_c$ additively, a change of $D$ by $n$ decades moves $B_c$ by $2.3\,n$: three decades give $\pm7$ out of $287$, six decades give $\pm14$ (5\%), and the prior mass of every regime changes by less than $10^{-3}$ over spreads of 0, 3 and 6 decades (Table 5). The semiclassical expansion is controlled by $1/B$, which is $3.5\times10^{-3}$ at $B_c$, so that higher-loop corrections change $\ln\Gamma$ by $O(1)$ and not $O(B)$, which the prefactor spread already covers. The regime in which the approximation fails is $B=O(1)$ to $O(10)$, where the wall is not thin on the scale of the field fluctuations; 0.5\% of the prior has $B<10$ and 2.5\% has $B<30$, and none of these draws survives the survival condition (all have $\Gamma/H^4\gg51$), so the failure of the semiclassical approximation affects only the excluded region and cannot change the classification of any draw that is allowed.

\subsection{Cooling and the thermally corrected potential}
The calculation above treats the universe as a clock: the CMB temperature $T_\gamma=T_0/a$ falls with the expansion and enters nothing. It can enter the decay only if the scalar interacts with a thermal bath, and then through a thermal correction to the potential whose coefficient $c$ is fixed by those interactions and is not a universal constant. Two regimes are therefore kept apart throughout: the scalar is decoupled and the decay is the zero-temperature O(4) channel of the previous sections, or it is in thermal equilibrium with the CMB, in which case the leading high-temperature term is

\begin{equation}
V_{\rm eff}(\varphi,T)=V_0(\varphi)+\tfrac12\,c\,T^2\varphi^2,\qquad \theta\equiv\frac{c\,T^2}{\lambda f^2}
\end{equation}
The thermal term is a rescaling of the zero-temperature family, which makes the analysis exact. With $x=sy$ and $s=\sqrt{1-2\theta}$ the potential $(x^2-1)^2/8+\eta x/2+\theta x^2/2$ becomes $s^4[(y^2-1)^2/8+(\eta/s^3)y/2]$ up to a constant. Hence a false minimum exists only for

\begin{equation}
\eta_{\rm eff}\equiv\frac{\eta}{(1-2\theta)^{3/2}}<\eta_c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\theta<\theta_c(\eta)=\frac12\left[1-\left(\frac{\eta}{\eta_c}\right)^{2/3}\right]
\end{equation}
and the bounce actions are those of the zero-temperature table at the shifted tilt: $B_4=b(\eta_{\rm eff})/\lambda$ and $S_3=(fs/\sqrt\lambda)\,s_3(\eta_{\rm eff})$, where $s_3=4\pi\int\rho^2[x'^2/2+w-w_f]d\rho$ is the O(3) action at $\lambda=1$, $\theta=0$ (tabulated here for the first time for this family, with the boundary-value solver for $\eta<0.1$, and tending to the thin-wall value $16\pi\sigma^3/3\Delta V^2$ as $\eta\to0$: the ratio is 0.995 at $\eta=0.03$). Direct solutions of the O(4) and O(3) bounce with the thermal term in the potential agree with these scalings to $2\times10^{-4}$ (check V19). For the benchmark $\lambda=1$, $\eta=0.30$: $f=3.05$ meV, $\theta_c=0.0765$, and today ($T_0=0.235$ meV) $\theta=0.0059\,c$.

Cooling relaxes the thermal term, and the thermal history has a consequence that does not depend on the decay rate: the false minimum disappears at $T_c=f\sqrt{\lambda\theta_c/c}$, which is 2.7 meV (redshift 10) for $c=0.1$ and 0.84 meV (redshift 2.6) for $c=1$. A scalar in equilibrium with the CMB at higher temperatures would have settled into the true vacuum at the symmetric-to-broken transition, and the present dark energy would not be the false-vacuum energy. The observed universe in the false vacuum therefore requires that the scalar either decoupled before $T_c$ or has $c<1.1\times10^{-5}$ if the bath is coupled all the way back to recombination ($z=1100$). The high-temperature form is an upper bound on the effect for particles heavier than $T$ in the loop, since the true correction is then Boltzmann suppressed; the results below are therefore the largest thermal effect that the coefficient $c$ can produce.

\subsection{The cooling history and the three decay channels}
The temperature follows the observed history, $T_\gamma(a)=T_0/a$ with $H(a)$ of Section 4.2, and cosmic time $t(a)-t_0=\int_1^a da'/[a'H(a')]$. Three channels compete. The Hawking--Moss transition is the horizon-scale channel of de Sitter space; its action is $\ln B_{\rm HM}\simeq279$ (Section 3.4) and it plays no role. The de Sitter horizon temperature $T_{\rm dS}=H_\Lambda/2\pi=1.91\times10^{-31}$ meV is $8.1\times10^{-31}$ of $T_0$, would equal the CMB temperature only after 69 e-folds ($\sim10^{12}$ yr), and is not a homogeneous radiation bath; it is therefore kept separate and not combined with $T_\gamma$ by a maximum. The thermal exponent at the horizon temperature, $S_3/T_{\rm dS}=4.2\times10^{32}$, rules out any activation by it. That leaves quantum tunneling (the O(4) bounce with action $B_4$) and thermal activation (the O(3) bounce with $S_3/T$). At a given temperature the saddle with the smaller exponent dominates, and the rate takes its exponent and prefactor; the two rates are not added, because they are the two ends of one family of periodic instantons. The thermal prefactor is $\Gamma_T=D_T\,T^4(S_3/2\pi T)^{3/2}e^{-S_3/T}$ with $D_T$ of order unity (a systematic of 1.5 decades, as for the quantum prefactor).

For the benchmark the crossover is at $T_\times=0.181$ meV, so that today the thermal saddle already dominates ($S_3/T_0=341<B_4=441$), the crossover being reached when the CMB has cooled by a factor 1.29, and in the past the thermal exponent was far smaller (Table 6, Fig. 6b). Cooling lowers the rate for $c\ge0$ at every epoch: the thermal exponent grows as $1/T$ and the thermal mass relaxes, so $\ln\Gamma$ falls monotonically with time (check V20). The late-time decay is therefore the zero-temperature O(4) decay of Section 4, because the thermal contribution switches off exponentially fast as $T\propto e^{-H_\Lambda t}$. For $c<0$ (symmetry non-restoration, outside the usual sign of the thermal mass) the barrier grows with $T$ and cooling lowers $B_4$, so the sign of the effect is reversed for the quantum channel, but the thermal exponent still has a minimum near $z\simeq2$ for $c=-1$ and the past rate is not small.

\section{What the nucleated region can become}
\subsection{Energy budget}
A thin-wall bubble of true vacuum is bounded by a wall that carries the released energy; its interior is, to leading order, the true vacuum with no excess energy to reheat. In the thick-wall regime relevant here the field at the centre of the bounce, $x_0$, lies above the true minimum, and the energy the interior can release is $\epsilon_{\rm in}\Delta V = V(x_0)-V_t$. Fig. 8c shows $\epsilon_{\rm in}$ against $\eta$: at $\eta=0.30$, $\epsilon_{\rm in}=0.018$, so 1.8\% of $\Delta V$ resides in the interior and the remainder in the wall; $\epsilon_{\rm in}$ approaches unity only near $\eta_c$, where the barrier is gone.

\subsection{Interior geometry and reheating}
The interior of a CDL bubble has open spatial slices, $ds^2=-dt^2+a(t)^2dH_3^2$ [23, 24], with the Friedmann constraint $(da/dt)^2 = 1 + a^2\rho/(3M_{\rm Pl}^2)$. Without a long inflationary stage inside the bubble (Section 5.3), the energy density is negligible against the curvature term at early times: $\alpha^2\rho \le 1.1\times10^{-61}$ in units where $H_\Lambda=1$, so the interior is a Milne universe, $a=t$. The condensate begins to oscillate at $t_0 = 1/m$ with $m = 3.61$ meV, behaves as matter and decays to radiation at a rate $\Gamma_\varphi\le m$. With $\tau=H_\Lambda t$ the radiation density has the closed form $\rho_r(\tau)=\epsilon\,\tau_0^3\,\gamma\,\tau^{-4}\int_{\tau_0}^{\tau}s\,e^{-\gamma(s-\tau_0)}ds$ in units of $V_f$, which we compare with a numerical integration of the full system (relative difference $6\times10^{-7}$). The radiation temperature is $T=(30\rho_r/\pi^2g_*)^{1/4}$ with $g_*=3.36$.

Energy conservation bounds the reheating temperature independently of the details of the decay,

\begin{equation}
T_{\rm reh}\le\left[\frac{30\,\epsilon_{\rm th}\,\epsilon_{\rm in}\Delta V}{\pi^2g_*}\right]^{1/4},\qquad \epsilon_{\rm th}\le1
\end{equation}
which for the largest release allowed when $V_t\ge0$, $\epsilon_{\rm in}\Delta V = \Delta V\le V_f=\rho_\Lambda$, gives $T_{\rm reh}\le 2.19$ meV ($25$ K), against the CMB temperature of 0.235 meV today and $4.6\times10^{8}$ times below the $\sim1$ MeV required for nucleosynthesis [42]. Equivalently, a thermal bath at 1 MeV needs an energy density $1.4\times10^{35}$ times the dark-energy density. The closed form shows that the situation is worse than the bound: the radiation temperature after its maximum falls as $T\propto1/\tau$ because the open interior keeps expanding, with $T\tau=5.46\times10^{-43}$ GeV, so $T=1$ MeV would have to occur at $t=3.0\times10^{-22}$ s, before the condensate even starts to oscillate ($t_0=1.8\times10^{-13}$ s). \subsection{Scope of the bound: what is excluded by the model and what only by energy conservation}
The bound of Eq. (13) is a statement about a locally homogeneous conversion of a vacuum-energy difference that does not exceed the dark-energy density. It is not a universal prohibition on high-temperature bubble interiors, and it is stated here with its assumptions: (A1) one real scalar field minimally coupled to gravity, with the potential of Section 2; (A2) the false-vacuum energy is the observed dark energy; (A3) the interior is homogeneous and isotropic on open slices; (A4) no inflationary stage inside the bubble; (A5) all energy densities are sub-Planckian. Models that violate one of these need a separate analysis. Table 9 lists the possible additional energy sources and says which of them the model excludes by more than energy conservation.

The geometric argument is the following. In the open slicing the curvature parameter $Y\equiv a^2\rho/(3M_{\rm Pl}^2)$ obeys $(da/dt)^2=1+Y$. A hot big bang at nucleosynthesis needs $Y\sim1$: in standard radiation domination $Ht=1/2$, that is $\rho t^2=3M_{\rm Pl}^2/4$. In the curvature-dominated phase $a=t$ and $Y=\rho t^2/(3M_{\rm Pl}^2)$ can only decrease, as $1/t$ for matter and $1/t^2$ for radiation, unless the interior inflates. The energy density of the condensate and its decay products obeys the envelope

\begin{equation}
\rho(t)\le\epsilon\,\Delta V\,(mt)^{-3}\quad(t>t_0=1/m),\qquad Y(t_0)=\frac{\epsilon\,\Delta V}{3M_{\rm Pl}^2m^2}
\end{equation}
because matter dilutes more slowly than radiation and decay only moves energy from the slower to the faster component (checked against the closed form to machine precision, check V17). The mass at the true minimum is $m=\kappa_m\Delta V^{1/4}$ with $\kappa_m=\lambda^{1/4}[(3x_t^2-1)/2]^{1/2}\delta^{-1/4}$, which lies between 0.50 and 4.1 over the whole prior, so $Y(t_0)=c\sqrt{\Delta V}/M_{\rm Pl}^2$ is tiny for any sub-Planckian energy: $Y(t_0)=2.0\times10^{-63}$ at $\Delta V=V_f$, and $Y(t_0)=1$ would need $\Delta V/M_{\rm Pl}^4=1.8\times10^{5}$ at the benchmark, which is super-Planckian. Therefore, for any $\Delta V$ below the Planck scale, the interior is a Milne universe at all times, $Y$ never approaches unity (Fig. 9), and the energy density at one second after nucleation cannot exceed $\epsilon\Delta V/(m\cdot1\,{\rm s})^3$. Requiring $\rho(1\ {\rm s})\ge\rho_{\rm BBN}=(\pi^2g_*/30)(1\ {\rm MeV})^4$ ($g_*=10.75$) demands $\Delta V^{1/4}\ge5.1\times10^{61}$ GeV, that is 42, 43 and 45 orders of magnitude above the Planck mass (for the smallest $\kappa_m$ in the prior, the benchmark and the largest). For the dark energy the envelope gives $\rho(1\ {\rm s})=4.4\times10^{-74}\,\rho_{\rm BBN}$.

Three points follow. First, the anti-de Sitter case shows that energy conservation alone is not sufficient: a very deep true vacuum releases enough energy for 1 MeV, but its open interior then collapses within seconds, and the longest-lived interior has $T\lesssim4$ meV. Second, the geometric statement is independent of the energy scale and of the value of $V_f$, and therefore covers an additional reservoir too: it cannot raise $Y$ above the curvature bound unless its energy density exceeds $\Delta V$ by $9.1\times10^{60}$. Third, the escape routes are exactly those the model lacks: a long inflationary stage inside the bubble, a parent energy scale close to the Planck scale, or an additional mechanism that raises $Y$ at $t_0$. A successful cosmology would have to lie in that outside class.

A second scalar field $\chi$ does not change this. For any number of scalar fields with a potential $V(\varphi,\chi)$, the energy released in the transition of a homogeneous region is the potential difference between the false and true vacua, $V_f-V_t$, and the false-vacuum energy $V_f=V(\varphi_f,\chi_f)$ is the whole observed dark energy $\rho_\Lambda$: any energy stored in $\chi$ while it sits in the false vacuum gravitates as dark energy and is already counted in $V_f$. A larger release needs $V_t<0$ (the anti-de Sitter row of Table 9, a crunch within seconds), and the curvature argument above is independent of the field content. A reservoir that is not vacuum energy (radiation, matter, kinetic energy of $\chi$) would have been present and gravitating throughout the cold era, where observations limit it to a fraction of the critical density, whereas the interior needs $\rho_x=9.1\times10^{60}\,\Delta V$. A coupled two-field model is therefore a relabelling of $\Delta V$ and not a source of new energy, unless the second sector inflates or carries a Planck-scale parent energy, which is the outside class of Section 7. The simulations of Section 5.5 confirm it.

\subsection{Other fates and the singularity question}
If the true vacuum has negative energy ($V_t<0$) the open interior collapses to a singularity in a finite time, $\tau_c = \pi/\sqrt{|V_t|/V_f}$: $|V_t|=0.01\,V_f$: 547 Gyr; $|V_t|=0.1\,V_f$: 173 Gyr; $|V_t|=1\,V_f$: 55 Gyr; $|V_t|=10\,V_f$: 17 Gyr. A decay to a de Sitter vacuum with $V_t>0$ leaves the interior with an accelerating phase and no radiation epoch. Only $V_t\simeq0$ leaves a regular, expanding, but empty interior. No case yields a nonsingular bounce from the parent geometry: the bubble is a region of the parent de Sitter space, causally connected to it, and the Milne interior is geodesically complete only because it has no matter.

The distinction between daughter regions and a cyclic cosmology (Section 1) applies to these fates. The nucleated region is a daughter region in the sense of Option A: a cold, curvature-dominated open region bounded by the wall inside the parent de Sitter space and still causally connected to it, not a new hot universe. The potential has no further metastable minimum at the true vacuum, so repeated transitions of the interior would need a different potential; the entropy produced in reheating is bounded by the energy $\Delta V$ and is carried by an open interior of infinite comoving volume, so no entropy statement can be made for the daughter as a closed system. A cyclic cosmology (Option B) needs a nonsingular bounce, which in spatially flat general relativity with conventional matter requires a violation of the null energy condition ($\dot H=-(\rho+p)/2M_{\rm Pl}^2>0$) and raises problems of stability and entropy [9, 10]; the present model contains no such mechanism. Entropy accounting and causality are computed for the daughter region in Section 5.5; the stability of an interior bounce is not.

\subsection{Lorentzian simulations of the daughter region: two fields, reservoirs and repeated transitions}
The interior was also simulated, not only bounded. The analytic continuation of the bounce gives the open homogeneous interior with the field starting at the bounce centre $x_0$ at rest and $a=t$ (Milne). In units of $f$ for the fields and $\lambda f^4$ for energy densities, with $g=f^2/M_{\rm Pl}^2$, the system is

\begin{equation}
\ddot x_i+\left(3\frac{\dot a}{a}+\Upsilon_i\right)\dot x_i+\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_i}=0,\qquad \dot\rho_r+4\frac{\dot a}{a}\rho_r=\sum_i\Upsilon_i\dot x_i^2,\qquad \ddot a=-\frac{g\,a}{6}(\rho+3p)
\end{equation}
with $\rho=\tfrac12\sum_i\dot x_i^2+V+\rho_r$, $p=\tfrac12\sum_i\dot x_i^2-V+\rho_r/3$ and the constraint $\dot a^2=1+g\,a^2\rho/3$, whose relative residual is the energy-conservation check (eighth-order Runge--Kutta, relative tolerance $10^{-11}$). The friction $\Upsilon_i$ is a phenomenological dissipation that moves field energy to radiation with total energy conserved. The simulations are the following (check V22--V24 and Table 10--13).

(A) Single field, physical benchmark ($\lambda=1$, $\eta=0.30$, $f=3.05$ meV, $g=1.6\times10^{-60}$). The full dynamics give a maximum radiation temperature within 7\% of the closed form of Section 5.2 for $\Gamma_\varphi/m$ from 1 to $10^{-3}$ (ratios 0.93, 1.04, 1.06 and 1.06), so the assumption that the condensate starts to oscillate at $1/m$ and dilutes as matter is sound. The constraint residual is at most $9\times10^{-9}$ for $g\ge10^{-3}$ (it scales with $g$ and is trivially small at $g=10^{-60}$, so the gravitational scan below is the real test). Scanning $g$ from $10^{-60}$ to $10^{3}$, the curvature parameter $Y=ga^2\rho/3$ reaches $3.9\times10^{-2}$ at $g=10^{1}$ and 0.48 at $g=10^{2}$; a flat-like radiation epoch ($Y\ge1/4$ with radiation dominant) first appears at $g=10^{2}$, that is $f=10\,M_{\rm Pl}$. For the dark-energy scale $g=10^{-60}$ and $Y_{\max}=3.9\times10^{-63}$.

(B) Two fields with an energy reservoir. A second field $\chi$ (here $V_\chi=\tfrac{\lambda_\chi}{4}(\chi^2-w_\chi^2)^2$ with $\lambda_\chi=1$, $w_\chi=1.5f$, coupled by $\tfrac12 g_c^2(\varphi-\varphi_t)^2\chi^2$ so that it is stable in the false vacuum and tachyonic in the true one) stores 1.27 $\lambda f^4$, 4.3 times the energy $\Delta V$ released by $\varphi$. A constant offset $V_{\rm off}=-(\Delta V+E_\chi)$ makes the false vacuum have the observed dark-energy density, which is the only way the reservoir can sit in the parent without gravitating. The true vacuum is then anti-de Sitter with $|V_t|=\Delta V+E_\chi$, and the simulation (dissipation $\Upsilon=m/2$ in both fields, $\chi$ seeded at $10^{-6}f$) transfers the reservoir to radiation. The depth is set in units of the dark energy, $|V_t|=\kappa\rho_\Lambda$, which fixes $f$ and $g$ (Table 10). For $\kappa=10^{35}$ (enough for 1 MeV) the radiation reaches 0.72 MeV after $8.9\times10^{-21}$ s, and the interior crunches after 5.46 s, equal to the pure anti-de Sitter value $\pi\sqrt3M_{\rm Pl}/\sqrt{|V_t|}$ to better than $10^{-12}$. The reservoir therefore does not make a hot, lasting epoch: it makes a hot instant followed by a crunch.

\section{Matter dilution and decoherence as a trigger}
The original motivation for a late-time transition was that dilution of matter reduces environmental decoherence and thereby permits a macroscopic quantum transition. In the baseline model nothing of the kind appears: the potential $V(\varphi)$, hence $B$, does not depend on the matter density, and the Gibbons--Hawking temperature $T_H=H/2\pi=1.9\times10^{-43}$ GeV [43] is $6\times10^{-32}$ of the field scale; thermal-type corrections to $B$ are of relative size $\sim g\,(\kappa\simeq3.5)$, that is $10^{-59}$ (Section 3.4). A dependence on matter must be introduced by a coupling, and a conclusion that held only for one coupling would be weak. The requirement can be stated without choosing one.

Any coupling of non-relativistic matter of mass $m_{\rm mat}(\varphi)$ adds $\rho_m\ln m_{\rm mat}(\varphi)$ to the effective potential, so that the energy difference between the vacua changes by $\rho_m\,\Delta\ln m$, where $\Delta\ln m$ is the fractional change of the matter mass between the two field values and $\rho_m=(\Omega_m/\Omega_\Lambda)V_f$ today. Write $\varepsilon_m\equiv\rho_m\Delta\ln m/\Delta V$ for the relative shift. To first order in the tilt shift $\Delta\eta$,

\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta\eta}{\eta}=\frac{2\,\delta(\eta)}{\eta\,(x_f-x_t)}\,\varepsilon_m,\qquad \Delta B=B\left|\frac{d\ln b}{d\ln\eta}\right|\frac{\Delta\eta}{\eta}
\end{equation}
with $\delta(\eta)=\Delta V/(\lambda f^4)$ of Eq. (3). A change of the rate by a factor $e$ needs $\Delta B=1$; moving a benchmark from $B=441$ to the critical action $B_c=287$ needs $\Delta B=154$ ($\Delta B=13.7$ for $\eta=0.32$). With $|d\ln b/d\ln\eta|=5.5$ at $\eta=0.30$ this gives $\Delta\ln m=8.9\times10^{-4}$ and $1.4\times10^{-1}$ respectively: the matter mass must differ by between about $0.1\%$ and $10\%$ between the two vacua. A noise-assisted mechanism would need an explicit open-system influence functional and a calculated change of the tunneling exponent, which is not assumed here.

\section{Consistency conditions and falsification tests}
A model of this class must satisfy internal consistency conditions, which are mathematical or follow from the framework, and it can be confronted with observational tests. The two are kept apart because failing the first means the model is ill-defined, while failing the second means that it is disfavored by data. All are computed in this paper and can be recomputed for a different potential with the supplied code.

\subsection{Internal consistency conditions}
\begin{itemize}\item C1 (existence). A metastable minimum requires $\eta<\eta_c=0.385$; for larger tilt there is nothing to decay.
\item C2 (semiclassical validity). The loop expansion parameter $1/B$ must be small. All draws with $B<30$ (2.5\% of the prior) have rates far above the survival bound, so the classification of allowed draws is unaffected (Section 4.3).
\item C3 (negative mode and flat limit). The bounce has one negative mode and gravity enters as an $O(g)$ perturbation, $g\sim10^{-60}$ (Section 3.3).
\item C4 (energy). With $V_t\ge0$ the released energy density cannot exceed $\rho_\Lambda$, so $T_{\rm reh}\le2.2$ meV; with $V_t<0$ the interior crunches (Section 5.3).
\item C5 (geometry). For any sub-Planckian energy the open interior stays curvature dominated, $Y(t)\le Y(t_0)=2.0\times10^{-63}$, unless it inflates; the single-field potential gives at most $9.3\times10^{-62}$ e-folds (Section 5.3).
\item C6 (percolation). A global transition requires $\Gamma/H^4=O(1)$ or larger, a window of width $\Delta B=3.9$ below the survival bound that holds 0.12\% of the prior volume.
\item C7 (thermal consistency). A scalar in equilibrium with the CMB at redshift $z$ keeps its false minimum only if $c<\theta_c\lambda f^2/T_\gamma(z)^2$, i.e. $c<1.1\times10^{-5}$ for a bath coupled back to recombination, and the survival condition then applies to the rate history $\Gamma(T_\gamma(t))$ (Section 4.8).
\item C8 (repeated transitions). Over any chain of transitions the energy released telescopes, $\sum_i\Delta V_i=\rho_\Lambda-V_{\rm final}\le\rho_\Lambda$, so a second generation cannot raise the temperature above $2.2$ meV (Section 5.5).
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Observational tests}
\begin{itemize}\item O1 (survival). The absence of a bubble in our past light cone requires $\Gamma V_4<3.0$, i.e. $\Gamma/H^4<51$ at 95\% confidence, which excludes 15\% of the prior volume of the benchmark family and constrains the future mean decay time only to $t_{\rm dec}\gtrsim8.2\times10^{7}$ yr.
\item O2 (reset window). A transition on cosmological time scales requires $B_c\lesssim B\lesssim B_c+28$ for decay within $10^{20}$ yr; this holds for 0.77\% of the prior volume. An action above $B_c+212$ puts the decay beyond $10^{100}$ yr.
\item O3 (hot epoch). A hot universe in the nucleated region requires $\rho_{\rm reh}\gtrsim(1\ \mathrm{MeV})^4$, which is $1.4\times10^{35}$ times the dark-energy density; the bound of Section 5 excludes it for decay of the present dark energy in this framework.
\item O4 (trigger). Matter dilution as the trigger needs $\Delta\ln m\gtrsim10^{-3}$ between the vacua, a linear coupling of $\beta\sim10^{26}$--$10^{29}$ or a screened coupling at a scale of tens of meV (Section 6), which local gravity and fifth-force tests can probe if the screening is incomplete.
\item O5 (signals). A solitary transition produces no signal in our patch; bubble collisions [25, 33, 34] require a population and $\Gamma/H^4=O(1)$ (C6). The vacuum is indistinguishable from a cosmological constant ($w=-1$) until it decays. A measured evolution of the dark energy, which recent baryon-acoustic-oscillation analyses combined with the CMB and supernovae prefer at the level of a few standard deviations depending on the supernova sample [44], would favor a rolling field and not a metastable vacuum. No observational discriminant of the cyclic reset is available from this model.
\item O6 (cooling). If the scalar is coupled to the CMB the decay probability was larger in the past and falls as the universe cools: equilibrated scenarios with $c\ge0$ lift the excluded prior mass from 15\% to 24--49\% and leave no survival-consistent draw that decays within $10^{20}$ yr, so the absence of a transition so far constrains the coupling of the scalar to the CMB as well as its potential (Section 4.8).
\end{itemize}
A transition producing a successful cosmology would have to lie outside the class studied here. It would need a parent vacuum energy of at least $\sim10^{34}\rho_\Lambda$ (an earlier, higher-energy phase of the universe rather than the present one) and, by Section 5.3, a post-nucleation region that inflates before it reheats, so that the curvature term and the horizon are handled, and a mechanism to restore the high-energy parent from the late universe. The cyclic models that have been constructed provide such mechanisms by other means [4, 5]; this paper shows that tunneling of the present dark energy does not.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\begin{itemize}\item A single scalar field and a quartic-tilt potential are used. Other potentials change $b(\eta)$ and the interior energy fraction, but not the scales: the rate depends on the combination $M_{\rm Pl}^4/V_f$ and the interior energy on $V_f$, both fixed by the data, so O1, O3 and the geometric argument of Section 5.3 are generic.
\item The one-loop prefactor is a systematic uncertainty, not a result; the conclusions are insensitive to it over six decades (Section 4.3).
\item The decay is treated as a flat-space process because $g\sim10^{-60}$; the gravitational code is validated against analytic limits and used for the map of the regime in which gravity matters, which the dark-energy scale excludes.
\item The survival criterion assumes a constant rate and a false vacuum throughout the history of the observed universe; a field that evolved or was thermally affected in the early universe would change the early part of the cone, which carries a small part of $V_4$.
\item The interior evolution assumes a homogeneous open slicing and a perturbative decay of the condensate to radiation; bubble-wall collisions can concentrate energy locally, but the average energy density is bounded by $\Delta V$ (Table 9).
\item Up-tunneling from the false to a higher vacuum, the decay of the true vacuum in curved space [45], thermal activation of the Hawking--Moss type [30] and decays of the present vacuum into other vacua are not considered.
\item The priors of the Monte Carlo are documented assumptions, not measurements; the regime fractions describe the sample space of the model, not the probability of a transition in our universe.
\item The matter-coupling analysis is first order in the tilt shift and does not simulate any specific screened model.
\item The thermal analysis assumes equilibrium with the CMB (an upper bound on the thermal channel), the leading high-temperature term with one coefficient $c$, and a bath coupled below a stated redshift; it neglects higher-order thermal corrections and the interpolation between the O(4) and O(3) saddles near $T_\times$, which a periodic-instanton calculation would refine.
\item The simulations of Section 5.5 are homogeneous and use a phenomenological dissipation: bubble-wall dynamics, inhomogeneous wall collisions, the microphysics of reheating and the gravitational backreaction of the wall are not simulated, and a nonsingular bounce (Option B) is not modelled.
\end{itemize}
\section{Conclusions}
A late-time false-vacuum transition of the present dark energy is a well-defined calculation, and for the explicit potential studied it can be done to a stated precision. The quantum decay is a flat-space process controlled by the action $B=b(\eta)/\lambda$, which must lie near $B_c\approx287$ for the transition to be neither excluded nor effectively absent; with survival evaluated on the past light cone the bound is weak ($\Gamma/H^4<51$) and the window is set by the action itself. Cosmological cooling does not help: a thermal mass rescales the potential exactly, the thermal O(3) channel dominates above 0.18 meV and falls monotonically as the universe cools, and a scalar in equilibrium with the CMB is more strongly constrained (24--49\% of the prior excluded against 15\%) with no late-time decay window left. The nucleated daughter region carries at most the dark-energy density, reheats to at most $2.2$ meV and cools as $1/t$ in a curvature-dominated open universe; this rests on geometry and not on energy conservation alone, holds for any sub-Planckian parent energy and any number of scalar fields, and a trigger by matter dilution would need a change of the matter mass between the vacua of at least $0.1\%$. A daughter region of the present dark energy is cold and empty, and the cyclic-reset hypothesis, in the form tested, is not supported; the framework identifies what would have to change.

Lorentzian simulations of the daughter region (Section 5.5) support these statements with two fields, dissipation and gravity: the closed-form interior is reproduced to 7\%, a reservoir hidden by a negative offset leaves an anti-de Sitter vacuum that crunches after 5.5 s when it reaches 0.7 MeV, flattening at the dark-energy scale needs a vev above $4M_{\rm Pl}$ and reheats below 1.5 meV, and repeated transitions release no more than $\rho_\Lambda$ in total. A viable hot daughter universe needs a parent vacuum energy at least $1.4\times10^{35}$ times the observed dark energy, which is excluded, or a cancellation of the same size, which ends in a crunch.
